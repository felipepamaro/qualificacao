\begin{center}
    \textbf{ABSTRACT}
    \vspace{10pt}
\end{center}

Between 2021 and 2025, the number of articles published in the proceedings of the Brazilian Computer Society (SBC) grew by approximately 81\%, while only 45.41\% of submitted works were accepted, indicating both an increase in scientific production and the selectivity of Brazilian conferences. In this scenario, the peer review process plays a significant role not only in evaluating works but also in training early-career researchers, by providing support for the improvement of their manuscripts. However, part of the knowledge required to produce an article that meets the expectations of a scientific community is not explicitly formalized. Writing and evaluation conventions are constructed and shared by the community and can be observed in published works, being gradually learned through experience and interaction with other researchers. Although large language models (LLMs) have been used for the analysis, generation, and evaluation of scientific texts, their use to identify and computationally represent these specific writing and evaluation conventions of a community or conference track and to use them as pre-submission support remains underexplored.

This research investigates how the genre conventions of a conference track can be derived from a corpus of accepted papers, computationally represented, and used by artificial intelligence (AI) agents to guide authors in preparing their manuscripts. To this end, we propose a software artifact based on AI agents, developed according to the principles of Design Science Research (DSR), which combines two complementary agents. The first is a genre compliance agent, responsible for analyzing a manuscript based on the genre specification derived from the corpus and producing actionable guidance. The second is a rejection boundary agent, designed to identify recurring problems and reasons for rejection from actual peer reviews of scientific papers, using this evidence to complement the genre analysis. The research will evaluate the validity of the diagnoses produced by the compliance agent, its ability to distinguish conventions across different tracks, and the perceived usefulness of the guidance provided to authors. As a contribution, this work is expected to provide evidence on the feasibility of transforming genre conventions into a computational representation capable of supporting manuscript preparation prior to submission.
